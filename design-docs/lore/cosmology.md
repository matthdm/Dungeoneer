# Dungeoneer — Cosmology

*The foundation of the world. Everything else is built on this.*

---

## Abaddon

Abaddon is not a god. He is the condition gods exist inside.

Entropy, dissolution, the patient unmaking of all things — these are not his domain. They are his nature. He predates the being that became God. He will outlast the last shard-holder. He is not malevolent. He does not pursue. He simply receives, eventually, everything.

His presence in the Dungeon is not dominion — it is gravity. The Dungeon exists inside him the way a cave exists inside a mountain. He is not aware of individual souls the way a person is aware of individual cells.

Until, perhaps, something unusual passes through.

He does not speak in the early game. When he finally does — after enough runs, enough deaths, enough cycles — he doesn't threaten. He asks questions. Quietly. As if he has all the time there is, which he does.

---

## The Unnamed

Before the Severance there was one organizing principle — not a person exactly, more like the weight that kept reality from flying apart. The closest word is God, but nothing in any collapsed world captures what it actually was. It had no name because nothing that contained it could name it.

Every civilization that survived its absence calls it something different. The most common translation across the Dungeon's fragments is *The Unnamed* — which is itself a kind of joke. You name the thing you no longer have, and also a wink the fact that every society calls "god" something different.

The Unnamed's order was real. Life thrived. Purpose existed. But it was absolute, and it was not asked for. Every soul had its place, its function, its end — written before it began. Some endured this gratefully. Others did not.

---

## The Severance

No one knows exactly how many acted. The histories disagree.

What is known: a number of the most powerful beings in existence — each chafing under a different aspect of the Unnamed's order — came together long enough to do one thing. Not to agree on what came after. Not to plan a better world. Just to end the one they had, and hope that *different* meant *better*.

They were not heroes. They were not tyrants. They were desperate and powerful and tired, and that combination is more dangerous than either heroism or evil.

The act is called **The Severance.** The beings who performed it are called **The Severed** — both because they severed the Unnamed, and because the act cut them from whatever they had been before.

Each absorbed a fragment of the Unnamed's infinite coherence. Each fragment was real power — real capacity to shape reality — but partial, colored by the holder's deepest nature, distorted by what *they* wanted different to mean. They did not collaborate on what came after. They barely agreed on the act itself.

They built worlds. Those worlds failed. What remains of those worlds is what the Dungeon is made of.

---

## Azazel

The Unnamed did not only organize. It also judged — not capriciously, but with the full weight of perfect knowledge applied with the certainty that only omniscience permits.

The instrument of that judgment was **Azazel**, made rather than born, purposed rather than choosing. He was not a god. He was a function made flesh — the Unnamed's hand of consequence.

When the Severance happened, Azazel was not unmade. He persisted. But his authority is now groundless. He punishes because that is the only thing he is. Without the Unnamed to define transgression, he defines it himself. Without divine wisdom to calibrate consequence, he applies it absolutely.

He is not cruel by choice. He is broken by design — executing a program whose source of authority no longer exists.

His presence in certain parts of the Dungeon is felt as weight. As guilt that attaches to things you did before you could have known they were wrong. He doesn't appear. He accumulates.

---

## The Dungeon

When a shard-world collapses, it doesn't disappear. It decays. Abaddon does not rush. The process is geological — civilization, then ruin, then sediment, then the Dungeon's floor. The deeper you descend, the older the collapse, the closer to the original act.

What the dungeoneer walks through is not a prison or a punishment. It is a graveyard of everyone's best idea about what the world should have been.

The souls caught in the Dungeon are not there because they sinned. They are there because their world ended and Abaddon's dissolution is slow, and in the meantime they have to be somewhere. Some have been here long enough to forget which world they came from. Some remember everything, and that is worse.

No one rules the Dungeon. No one built it as a place. It accumulated, the way sediment does — not by design, but because there was nowhere else for what the Severance produced to go.

## Shard-Power and Artifacts

When a Severed's world collapsed, their shard-power did not disappear. Power at that scale does not disappear. It dispersed — into the physical matter of the ruined world. The stone, the architecture, the weapons, the relics. Everything built during a civilization's peak carries trace fragments of its shard. The deeper the ruin, the denser the concentration.

Legendary artifacts are not well-made weapons. They are fragments of a dead god-killer's divine inheritance, crystallized into physical form over centuries of geological compression. When you equip something from The Bound, you are channeling a piece of Solen's shard. Build around one domain's artifacts and you are effectively reconstructing one Severed's vision of the world inside your own body — not as metaphor, but as the actual mechanism by which their power flows.

Most of the power dispersed into objects. But not all. Some fragments seek hosts — living minds complex enough to hold them. This is why the major NPC inhabitants of the Dungeon are more powerful than ordinary souls. They carry micro-fragments of their domain's shard, absorbed through proximity, devotion, or survival. Most don't fully know this.

The player, without understanding it, is helping consolidate shard-power into living hosts. Every task completed for an NPC, every item given, every act of trust — these transfer fragments from the ruins into the NPC. By the time an NPC ascends to boss, they have consolidated enough shard-power to be genuinely divine in scale. The player helped build the god they now have to kill.

Abaddon receives all of it eventually. Slowly.

---

## The Shape of a Run

Each floor the player descends is a layer of geological lore — sediment from a specific collapsed domain, older and denser the further down. Biomes are not aesthetic choices. They are ruins. The enemies are what the inhabitants became. The architecture is what the civilization left. The artifacts are what the philosophy produced at its peak.

The player is not exploring a dungeon. They are excavating the corpse of the world.
