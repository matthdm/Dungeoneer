# Dungeoneer — The Severance Accounts

*What follows is reconstructed from shard-residue, Dungeon fragments, and the partial testimonies of souls who were present or adjacent. No account is complete. None can be verified against another. They agree on almost nothing except the act itself, and even on that they cannot agree on the cost.*

*These are not confessions. Most of the people who did this believed they were right. Some of them still do.*

---

## I. Solen — The Lawbringer

**Source:** A letter recovered from the deep Catacomb sediment. The author is not named but the handwriting matches records of Solen's civil correspondence. No recipient is identified.

---

I have been asked, more than once, whether I knew what I was doing.

Yes. This is the only honest answer. The others may tell you differently — that we acted on desperation, on grief, that the moment arrived before the decision did. That is not false. But it is incomplete.

I knew. I knew because the question I had been carrying for three hundred years was finally answerable: *does the Unnamed consent to this?* And the answer was that the Unnamed had never concerned itself with consent in either direction. It didn't ask for our obedience. It did not need to ask for our act.

When the chain-law is incomplete — when two precedents conflict and the court cannot reconcile them — there is a procedure. You construct the principle that both rulings are imperfect applications of. You apply the principle. You let the lesser laws follow from it.

The Unnamed's order was law without principle. Rules without the thing rules are supposed to produce. Every soul in its place, every function executing, every purpose fulfilled — and beneath it all, no answer to *why*, only *because*. A chain that runs to nothing. A contract with no party that can be called to account.

What I wanted to build, after, was the opposite. The binding you choose. The chain that goes somewhere.

I did not know it would fail. I believed it could hold.

I am aware of what the Catacomb became. The chain still runs to nothing. I have had time to think about why.

The chain law is not the chain. The chain is the people who agree to carry it. I built the law. I did not build the people. Or rather — I tried to. I believed the structure would produce the virtue. I was wrong about which direction that goes.

I was right to end the old order. I do not know anymore if I was right to build the replacement I built. That is not the same admission. I want to be precise. I was not wrong to act. I may have been wrong about what acting would produce.

That is the distinction I have been working on for a very long time.

---

## II. Ciara — The Brighthand

**Source:** Spoken account, recorded by an unnamed Pyre historian. The framing is a conversation that allegedly occurred three days before the Severance.

---

Someone asked me: what do you do when you're afraid?

I burn it.

They thought I meant the fear. I didn't mean the fear. I meant the thing that's holding the fear still long enough for it to accumulate. Fear that moves is just motion. Fear that sits becomes a weight you can't put down.

I told them: you burn the container. Not to destroy what's inside — to let it move.

They asked me what was inside the thing I was afraid of. I said: nothing that can't become something else. That's the point. Everything can become something else. Everything can become something that wasn't suffocating.

Three days later we did it.

I want to tell you it felt like fire. That would be satisfying. That would be coherent. The truth is it felt like putting down a weight I had been carrying so long I had stopped noticing the posture it required. Like suddenly standing straight after years of compensating for something I couldn't name.

And then the world opened and we each took our piece of it and went off to build.

I built something that burned. This is what I believed: the burning is not the point. The burning is the precondition. What comes after the ash is what I was building toward.

The ash had no seeds left. I don't fully understand how that happened. I have theories. The shard pulled harder than I expected. The people I made were better at burning than at what came after. I think — I have thought about this for a very long time — that I made it too easy to be strong at the wrong time. Power came through destruction. The people who had the most power were the ones who had destroyed the most. What do strong destroyers build?

They build more burning.

I knew fire had to be pointed. I did not build a world where pointing it was honored the way using it was. That is my error. Not the Severance. The architecture after.

I would do the Severance again. I would build something different from it.

---

## III. Thessaly — The Unmasked

**Source:** A philosophical text found in Mirror Halls sediment, in multiple handwritten copies. Whether it was written before or after the Severance is disputed. The argument it makes is not hypothetical.

---

The question is not: was it right to act?

The question is: what makes an action right?

If rightness comes from the Unnamed — if the Unnamed's perfect knowledge is the only reliable arbiter of correct action — then no, it was not right. Nothing performed in defiance of perfect knowledge is right by the terms of that knowledge. I accept this. It does not concern me.

If rightness is something a self makes — if the act of choosing and committing is where value is generated, not received — then the Severance was the first genuinely right action in the history of existence. It was the act by which beings with selves *chose*. Not the outcomes. Not the plan. Not the destination. The *choosing*.

This is what I told the others. Most of them heard it as permission. I meant it as description.

Here is the thing about selfhood: it does not guarantee good choices. It guarantees *choices*. You cannot have sovereignty over your own form and also have someone else be responsible for what you build with it.

My world failed because the selves within it were genuinely sovereign and genuinely incompatible. I knew this was possible. I believed that genuine contact between sovereign selves — even painful contact, even irreconcilable contact — was better than the alternative. The Unnamed's order had no contact. It had function. Everyone in their place, everyone fulfilling purpose, and beneath it all, the hollow ache of never having actually *met* anyone, only encountered their assigned role.

What I wanted was the meeting. Real meeting. Where two things that are genuinely different try to occupy the same space.

I know what the Mirror Halls became. I know what irreconcilable sovereign selves do when they can each assert their truth at full force.

I would still choose contact over assignment. I am no longer certain this choice was made *for* the people I made it for, or for myself. Thessaly wanted to not be fixed. Thessaly built a world where nothing was fixed. These are not the same thing as giving other selves the same freedom.

That is the question I have not answered.

Mira is asking the same question with different tools.

---

## IV. Aldren — The Rootspeaker

**Source:** Not a text. A pattern in root-growth recovered from deep Moss sediment that apparently recurs across seventeen distinct excavation sites. The Dungeon's archivists believe it may be intentional. The pattern, translated, reads:*

---

I was the only one who didn't argue about what came after.

The others wanted to replace one thing with another. They had a destination. They built toward it. They were wrong about the destination, and now those worlds are the Dungeon's ceiling.

I wanted to remove the hand. That's all. Remove what was imposed and see what grew.

I don't regret this. I want to say that plainly because everything that follows sounds like regret and it isn't. Regret requires that I would have chosen differently. I wouldn't. The Unnamed's hand was on the scale, and it should not have been on the scale, and removing it was correct.

What grew without the hand was not what I expected. I expected competition. I expected the honest dominance hierarchy of a real ecology. I expected that things would find their level and that the level would be genuine rather than assigned.

The thing that found its level was the thing that could eat everything else.

I had not considered that honest competition includes honest dominance. And honest dominance, with nothing to balance it, is just dominance. The ecology I trusted collapsed into a singularity. One thing ate the rest and then starved.

The lesson I drew was: balance requires a gardener.

Kael drew the same lesson. Kael is applying it in the Dungeon. I have watched him. He is not wrong. He is also, if I am being honest with the root-pattern, becoming the thing I tried to remove. The gardener who decides what grows and what doesn't is a hand on a scale. I know this. Kael knows this. He has decided it is a necessary hand.

Maybe it is. Maybe the choice is: hand on the scale, or everything eaten.

Maybe I should have asked that question before the Severance instead of after.

But I'll tell you something the others won't: I didn't trust the Unnamed not because I had a better plan. I didn't trust it because it was in the way of things finding out what they were. I still think that. I still think knowing what you are — really knowing, through contact and consequence and the honest risk of being wrong — is worth something. Even if what you find out is that you're the thing that eats everything.

The knowing is real.

I am not sure the others ever wanted knowing. They wanted their version of the world. I wanted the world to be able to have versions.

I may have been wrong about what that produces.

I am not wrong that it was worth the attempt.

---

## V. Vael — The Stillkeeper

**Source:** A preservation vessel found in Cocytus sediment. The vessel is empty. The inscription on its exterior, in a script predating all known Pale language, is believed to be Vael's own writing. It is the only known instance of him writing from outside the preservation framework.*

---

I preserved everything.

I mean this as a confession and not as a boast, which is how it always sounded when I said it before. I preserved everything because everything was worth preserving. The world at the Unnamed's high point, before entropy had its way, before the wheel turned past the good parts — I wanted to hold it. I wanted to say: *here is what was worth it. Here is what we should not lose.*

The others wanted different. I wanted *more*. More of the good moments. More time with the things worth having.

I knew I was participating in ending something. I am not sure I fully understood that I was participating in ending everything. I believed the Unnamed's coherence would distribute into the six of us and the six of us would rebuild separately, and what we built would be better or at least *chosen*, and then we would preserve the chosen thing.

The Pale was an archive. The most complete archive ever assembled. Every good thing, held.

When the shard failed, it failed all at once. A civilization's worth of accumulated entropy arrived simultaneously, and everything I had held dissolved in what felt like a single moment. I was not ready for this. I had preserved the things but I had not prepared the people for what it meant that the things could be lost. They had lived for generations in a world where nothing worth having was ever lost. They did not know how to absorb loss. They dissolved with the archive.

I am in the Dungeon now in the same sense that the preservation vessels are in the Dungeon — a container that remembers holding something. The Hollow Monk performs my rituals because the ritual is still there and he is still there and neither of us has found a reason to stop, because stopping would require agreeing that what we preserved is gone, and I have not made that agreement.

I was right that it was worth preserving. I was wrong that preservation was possible.

These are not contradictions. They are the two things I am made of now.

## VI. Carath — The Hungering

**Source:** Nothing written. No account was recovered. What follows is reconstructed by Dungeon archivists from the testimonies of three hundred and twelve souls who encountered Carath in the period between the Severance and the Endless's collapse. The reconstruction is disputed. The archivists believe it represents what he would have said if he had ever stopped wanting long enough to describe the wanting.*

---

*He wanted the Severance the way he wanted everything: urgently, specifically, with complete inability to imagine not wanting it.*

*He did not, as far as anyone could determine, think about what came after. Not because he was stupid — he wasn't — but because thinking about after required not wanting something yet, and he had never not wanted something.*

*He told a soul named Varos, three days after the act: "I did it because I couldn't not. That's the point. That's always been the point. You do the things you can't not do and the things you can't not do are the things you actually are."*

*Varos asked him if the Unnamed had been able to not do anything.*

*Carath said he didn't know. He said he'd never thought about it. He said he was already thinking about something else.*

*The archivists note that this is probably the most honest account of the Severance that exists. The others have had time to build frameworks around their motivations. Carath never did. He wanted it. He did it. He wanted the next thing.*

*The Endless ate itself because Carath built a world in his own image and his image was: want more, always, faster, and do not think about the shape of what is left after you have had it.*

*He may not have been wrong that the wanting is the engine. He may have been wrong that engines don't need governors.*

*He may not have had the capacity to think about governors. That is the saddest version.*

*Most of the archivists believe the saddest version.*

---

## Coda

A minor archivist from the Pale — eight hundred cycles into Dungeon residency, which is old enough to have pieced most of this together herself — spent a significant portion of her existence cross-referencing these accounts. She is the primary reason they survived in recoverable form.

Her annotation at the end of the last compilation:

*Each of them understood their grievance with perfect clarity. Each built something that was the precise shape of that understanding. None of them appear to have asked whether the grievance, however accurate, was sufficient architecture for a world.*

*Solen was right that the Unnamed's order was unearned. His chains were no less unearned. Ciara was right that stasis was suffocating. Her fire had the same appetite. Thessaly was right that fixed identity was a cage. Her alternative dissolved the ground selves stand on. Aldren was right that imposed hierarchy was dishonest. He trusted honest hierarchy to self-correct. Vael was right that beautiful things decay. He solved decay by stopping time, which is not the same thing as solving it. Carath was right that desire is generative. He may have been the most correct of any of them, and his world still ate itself.*

*They were not wrong about the problem. I have spent eight hundred cycles trying to find the formulation where this goes differently, where one of them was wrong about the problem and that's what saved them. It doesn't go differently. They were right about the problem. The problem is that being right about the problem is not the same as knowing what to build.*

*I don't know what to build either. I still do the rites.*

The compilation ends there. The archivist is still in the Dungeon. She performs preservation rites over empty vessels with nothing left in them, because the rites are the only true thing she has left and she is not ready to put them down.

Abaddon is aware of her, as he is aware of everything inside him. She is progressing at the rate expected. He is not impatient. He has never been impatient about anything, including this.
